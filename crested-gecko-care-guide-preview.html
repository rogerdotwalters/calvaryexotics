Is a crested gecko right for you?

Crested geckos are one of the most beginner-friendly reptiles. They do well at normal household temperatures, eat a powdered diet you mix with water, and are generally calm once settled in. They are also long-lived, so think of one as a 15–20 year pet rather than a short-term project.

They are nocturnal, which means they are most active after the lights go down. They are also jumpy: a crested gecko is more likely to leap than to bite. If you want a pet you can watch in the evening and handle gently in short sessions, this is a great species. If you want a daytime, hands-on animal, a different species may suit you better.

Enclosure

Crested geckos are arboreal, so height matters more than floor space. Choose a tall, front-opening or well-ventilated enclosure:

  • Juveniles: a 12×12×18 in enclosure is a good starting point. Very small hatchlings can start in a smaller setup so they find their food easily.
  • Adults: 18×18×24 in is the common minimum. Bigger is fine and gives you more room for plants.

Look for a screen or mesh top, or ventilation on at least two sides. Stale, wet air is a bigger problem than dry air. Fill the enclosure with climbing and hiding space at several heights:

  • Cork bark flats and tubes
  • Horizontal and diagonal branches or vines
  • Plenty of foliage, live or artificial, for cover along the sides and top
  • A ledge or feeding dish up off the floor, where the gecko spends most of its time

Keep one gecko per enclosure. Males fight, and even females can stress each other and compete for food.

Substrate & bioactive setup

The easiest options are paper towel for hatchlings and new arrivals (simple to keep clean and to spot droppings) and coconut fiber or a bioactive mix for an established gecko.

A bioactive setup uses live plants and a cleanup crew of isopods and springtails that break down waste and keep the enclosure healthy. It takes a little more work up front, and it is what we use. Our bioactive supplies page covers substrate, springtails, and cleanup crews.

Whatever you choose, avoid sand, gravel, and fine loose substrate for hatchlings, and never use cedar or pine shavings.

Temperature & lighting

This is where crested geckos are easy: room temperature is usually right. Aim for 72–78°F during the day, with a slight drop at night into the upper 60s to low 70s. They are more at risk from overheating than from being too cool, and sustained temperatures above 80°F cause stress. Put a thermometer at the level where the gecko sits instead of guessing.

Most homes in Texas need no heat lamp, and in summer the concern is keeping the enclosure cool. Keep it away from sunny windows, and if your room runs hot, move the enclosure to a cooler spot.

Lighting

  • Give them a regular day and night cycle: about 10–12 hours of light, then darkness. A simple timer helps.
  • UVB is optional. Many keepers raise healthy geckos on a complete diet without it. Low-level UVB (around 2–5%) is considered helpful by many keepers, as long as there is plenty of shade and foliage so the gecko can avoid it.

Heat warning: never place a crested gecko enclosure in direct sun or next to a vent, heater, or electronics that run warm. Overheating is the most common way a healthy gecko gets sick.

Humidity & water

Crested geckos want a daily humidity cycle rather than constant dampness:

  1. Mist in the evening until the leaves and walls are wet and humidity climbs to about 70–80%. Geckos drink the droplets.
  2. Let it dry back to about 50–60% during the day. Good ventilation does this for you.

Use dechlorinated or filtered water, and a digital hygrometer you can trust. Offer a shallow water dish too, kept clean. If the enclosure stays soaked all day or smells musty, you need more ventilation, not more misting.

Feeding

The staple is a complete prepared crested gecko diet, a powder you mix with water into a thin paste or ketchup-like consistency. These are formulated to cover the nutrition crested geckos need, and they are the foundation of our feeding routine.

  • Juveniles: offer fresh food every day or every other day.
  • Adults: two to three times a week is usually enough.
  • Serve in a small dish mounted up off the floor, and remove leftovers after about 24 hours since mixed food spoils.
  • Insects are optional. A few gut-loaded dubia roaches or crickets once a week or two make good enrichment. Dust them lightly with calcium if you feed them.
  • Fruit baby food and fresh fruit are not needed, and too much sugar is not good for them.

Crested geckos are hard to overfeed on the prepared diet, but you should still watch their body condition. A healthy gecko has a plump tail base and no visible hip bones, and your eyes and a gram scale will catch changes before they become a problem.

Handling & taming

  1. Give a new gecko one to two weeks to settle in before handling. Make sure it is eating and drinking first.
  2. Start with short sessions of 5 to 10 minutes, in the evening when it is awake.
  3. Keep low. Sit on the floor or over a bed or sofa so a jump does not become a fall.
  4. Let it walk hand to hand instead of gripping it. Never grab or pull on the tail.

Crested geckos can drop their tail when frightened, and it does not grow back. A tailless gecko lives a normal life, but it is best avoided with calm, low handling. Skip handling during shedding, and give them time on their own if they seem stressed.

Shedding & health

A healthy crested gecko has clear eyes, a plump tail base, and a steady appetite. Geckos shed in pieces and usually eat the skin, so you may not see it. High humidity during the evening misting helps shedding go smoothly.

Signs to watch for

  • Stuck shed on toes or the tail tip: raise humidity and see a vet if it does not clear, since it can cut off circulation.
  • Sunken or wrinkled eyes and skin: dehydration. Check water, misting, and temperature.
  • Weight loss or refusing food for more than a couple of weeks.
  • Bent jaw or limbs, a kinked spine or tail, or tremors: possible metabolic bone disease from poor nutrition.
  • Bubbles at the nose, wheezing, or open-mouth breathing: possible respiratory infection.
  • Runny or unusually foul droppings that continue for more than a few days.

This guide is general information, not veterinary advice. If your gecko shows any of the signs above, or you are unsure, contact an exotic veterinarian. Early care makes a big difference.

A yearly checkup, including a fecal test, is a good habit. Whenever you bring home a new animal, keep it in a separate room from your others for a quarantine period.

Crested gecko care FAQ

How big do crested geckos get?

Adults usually reach about 8 inches including the tail and weigh roughly 35 to 55 grams. Most reach adult size at 18 to 24 months.

How long do crested geckos live?

With good care, 15 to 20 years is typical, so plan for a long commitment.

Do crested geckos need UVB?

Not strictly. Many keepers raise healthy crested geckos on a complete prepared diet without UVB. Low-level UVB (around 2 to 5 percent) with plenty of shade and foliage is considered beneficial by many keepers, and it is easy to add if you can set it up safely.

How often should I feed a crested gecko?

Juveniles do best with fresh food every day or every other day. Adults are usually fed two to three times a week. Remove uneaten food after about 24 hours.

Can I keep two crested geckos together?

It is safest to house each gecko alone. Males will fight, and even females can stress or injure each other and compete for food. A single gecko per enclosure also makes it easy to track eating and droppings.

Do crested geckos bite?

Rarely, and a bite from a crested gecko is a light pinch. They are more likely to jump or lose their tail when frightened, so handle them low to the ground over a soft surface.

Why is my crested gecko not eating?

A new gecko may take a week or two to settle in. Check that temperatures are between 72 and 78 degrees Fahrenheit, that the gecko is hydrated, and that it is not about to shed. Weight loss, sunken eyes, or refusing food for more than a couple of weeks calls for an exotic vet.

Do crested geckos regrow their tails?

No. Once a crested gecko drops its tail it does not grow back. Tailless geckos live normal, healthy lives and are sometimes called frog butts.
